What changed in four months

Unitree listed publicly, large funding deals were announced, and workers protested a planned humanoid deployment. Investment is growing, but most humanoid robots are not yet deployed at scale.

Examples: listings, funding, and orders

Public listings and proposed mergers. Unitree listed on Shanghai’s STAR Market on Aug 19. Its shares were priced at ¥150.80, opened 629% above that price, and closed the day up about 460%. Reports put its peak market value at roughly $53–62B; estimates vary with exchange rates and timing. The IPO raised about ¥6.1B (roughly $900M), though earlier coverage cited a planned ¥4.2B offer. In the US, Agility Robotics announced a proposed $2.5B merger with Churchill Capital Corp XI, a special-purpose acquisition company (SPAC), on June 24. The deal was expected to provide more than $620M, including a roughly $200M PIPE, but had not closed as of September 8. Other Chinese robotics firms have begun or are considering listing processes: AgiBot, EngineAI, Leju, RobotEra, and LimX among them. 36Kr counted 45 Chinese robotics companies in IPO processes.

Several companies announced unusually large funding rounds. Anduril closed a $5B round at a $61B valuation (May 13); Helsing closed a $1.8B round at about $18B (Jul 13); Hadrian raised $1.37B (Aug 6); Quantum Systems raised $1.2B (Jul 2); NEURA Robotics announced a Series C of up to $1.4B, though the committed amount is undisclosed; XPeng spun out its robotics unit with more than $900M (Aug 24); and Generalist AI raised $400M in June and announced a roughly $200M extension in August. These firms work across robotics, manufacturing, and defense, so funding totals depend on how a source defines the sector. Crunchbase counted $18.8B in robotics startup funding in H1 2026, more than its full-year 2025 total, and a record $3.79B humanoid quarter in Q2. Figure also announced a compute agreement with Nscale: $3.5B in planned cloud compute, with an option to scale beyond $6B. The GPUs are expected to come online from H2 2027; this is a compute commitment, not equity funding.

Pilots started converting to contracted revenue. Agility reported >$300M in multi-year orders for Digit v5 alongside its SPAC filing, across 9 live customer sites. Figure moved to multi-station production work at BMW (below). UBTech posted ¥1.27B (~$189M) H1 revenue, +104% YoY, with 921 full-size humanoids sold in the half. Skeptics’ note: much of the Chinese order book is government/SOE procurement, and almost none of these figures are independently audited.

Deployment: what moved and what stalled

Some robots entered production or paid service; many humanoids remain in pilots or are unverified. Worker protests added a labor issue to deployment plans.

Deployment examples and sources

Moved to production. Figure 03 started parts-sequencing work at BMW Spartanburg (Hall 52; announced May 13, first logistics workflow demoed Jun 30), with BMW Leipzig transitioning from test operation to an official pilot over the summer — Figure’s first EU site. Figure also built its 1,000th Figure 03 in late July and ran an approximately 200-hour unattended sorting livestream. The package count is disputed (12,732 per one tracker versus about 250,000 per a Chinese aggregator), and no third party audited it. Agility’s Digit now runs at nine customer sites (Schaeffler, GXO, Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada, and Mercado Libre); UBTech reported selling 921 full-size humanoids in H1. Zoox began charging fares in Las Vegas on Aug 10 in a driverless vehicle without a steering wheel. FedEx put Dexterity’s trailer-loading robots into production at its Hagerstown, Maryland hub (Jul 30), while UPS ordered 400 Pickle Robot unloaders for deployment starting in late 2026. Warehouse operators also reported increased scale: Symbotic recorded two consecutive GAAP-profitable quarters and a $22.5B backlog; Locus Robotics passed 7 billion lifetime picks; Amazon’s fulfillment network passed 1 million mobile robots (mid-2025, before this reporting window).

Slipped or still unproven. Tesla’s Optimus V3 remains unrevealed (targeted Q1, then “mid-2026,” now undated); the Q2 call (Jul 22) introduced the “Optimus Academy” reframe — first units are internal data collectors, not workers — dropped the word “volume” from production language, and pushed meaningful volumes (thousands) to H1 2027. Yahoo Finance reported in Jun that zero Optimus units do useful work in Tesla factories. 1X opened its Hayward NEO factory (Apr 30, pre-window edge) but no independently verified NEO home delivery exists as of mid-Jul, despite May reports that shipping had begun; its reported $1B round at $10B+ never confirmed closed. Kodiak’s long-haul driverless launch, promised “later this year,” has not been confirmed as of Sep 8. And the widely repeated “UPS × Figure” deal remains unconfirmed — no contract or deployment has surfaced.

Planned deployment prompted worker protests. On Jul 16–17, Hyundai’s Ulsan workers staged rolling strikes, described as the auto industry’s first factory stoppage explicitly over humanoid deployment. They demanded union approval rights before Atlas robots enter Korean plants. Hyundai has committed to 25,000 Atlas units across its factories and plans a production system capable of 30,000 units a year by 2028. All 2026 Atlas output is allocated to Hyundai’s RMAC data facility and Google DeepMind. The dispute shows that workforce consultation may affect where and when robots are deployed.

Listings and consolidation

Unitree listed in Shanghai. Other companies proposed listings or mergers, while some sold or closed their robotics businesses.

Company listings and acquisitions

The queue. Unitree’s Aug 19 debut (fastest CSRC approval in STAR history — 104 days) is the benchmark every Chinese robotics IPO now prices against. In-window listings: Momenta raised ~$752M on HKEX (Jul 8, 414× oversubscribed) and Robotphoenix ~$86M (May, +80% first day). Filed or in process: AgiBot, EngineAI (confidential), Leju, DEEP Robotics, RobotEra, Galbot (banks appointed), X Square Robot (confidential, ~Sep 1) — 45 companies total per 36kr. US pure-play robotics S-1s remain 12–18 months out; the American exit paths opened this window were SPACs: Agility, PlusAI ($800M, its third attempt, Sep 3), FORT Robotics (~$560M, Aug), Powerus, and Matternet’s go-public transaction.

The acquirers. NVIDIA confirmed it is buying Hugging Face for ~$13B (Sep 3; $11.9B per the 8-K plus ~$1B earmarked) — sweeping the leading open-source robotics stack (LeRobot, Reachy Mini, Microduck) into the chip incumbent. Meta acquired humanoid-AI startup Assured Robot Intelligence (May) into Superintelligence Labs. Hyundai bought SoftBank’s remaining 9.65% of Boston Dynamics for ~$325M (announced Jun, completed ~Jul 16–21), making BD wholly Hyundai-owned and teeing up a Nasdaq IPO for 2027–28. SoftBank’s $5.375B ABB Robotics acquisition is EU-cleared (Mar 12) and financed ($1.75B loan, Jul 22) but had not closed as of Sep 8; SoftBank is also reportedly in talks for a majority of 1X at ~$6B (Aug 26–27 — a markdown from the $10B+ 1X sought). Pre-window, included for completeness: Amazon bought RIVR and Fauna Robotics (Mar), Mobileye acquired Mentee Robotics for $900M (Jan), Skild AI absorbed Zebra’s Fetch robotics business (Apr 15), Symbotic bought Fox Robotics (Feb), and Serve Robotics bought Diligent Robotics ($29M, closed late Jan).

The shakeout. iRobot’s saga resolved: Chapter 11 (Dec 14, 2025) ended with contract manufacturer Shenzhen Picea taking 100% of equity (Jan 23, 2026) — the Roomba is now a Chinese-owned value brand, and it shipped two product waves this summer (May 12, Jul 7). Vicarious Surgical’s shareholders voted to dissolve and liquidate (Jul 21) — the same week J&J’s Ottava won FDA authorization. Karl Storz exited surgical robotics hardware and retired the Asensus brand (Jun 17). Ekso Bionics was reverse-merged into an AI-cloud company (ChronoScale, May 5) with its exoskeleton business held for sale. German Bionic, insolvent since Nov 2025, was acquired by Archimedes Partners (Mar 3). Cartwheel Robotics went bankrupt (May); mower startup Graze filed for bankruptcy; K-Scale Labs shut down (Nov 2025, pre-window — the May edition missed it). Even the survivors show stress: Locus Robotics raised at $1.35B, down from ~$2B in 2022, and subsea’s Nauticus is in a liquidity crisis ($0.9M Q2 revenue, $2M cash).

Robotaxis & fleets

Zoox and Tesla began paid robotaxi service under different US approval paths. Safety incidents and recalls continued.

Fleet updates and incident details

Zoox took the exemption route; Tesla took self-certification; both are now commercial. NHTSA granted Zoox the first-ever Part 555 exemption for paid service with no manual controls (Jul 30; up to 2,500 vehicles/yr for two years), and paid Las Vegas rides began Aug 10 — after a Jun scare in which an unoccupied Zoox drove into heavy smoke at an active fire scene and the whole 105-vehicle fleet was recalled for a software update. Tesla launched paid public Cybercab rides in Austin on Sep 4 — and NHTSA opened Audit Query AQ26002 into ~1,000 Cybercabs the same day, probing Tesla’s FMVSS self-certification of a vehicle with no wheel or pedals. Tesla’s robotaxi service is otherwise live in 6–7 metros across three states, with a Nevada permit for Las Vegas (sources conflict: 5,000-vehicle permit ceiling vs ~2,500 in year one).

Waymo changed cadence — and had its hardest summer on safety. Four cities announced in one day (Jul 8), three opened simultaneously (Sep 1): 14 US metros, ~500K paid rides/week, a stated 1M/week year-end target, and Chinese-built Zeekr robotaxis now carrying public passengers in Denver and San Diego. Counterweight: two in-window recalls (3,791 units for flooded-road driving; 3,871 for entering closed freeway construction zones), a fatal Dallas crash (Aug 7–8) in which a pedestrian struck by a human-driven SUV was thrown into a driverless Waymo’s path, and still-open NHTSA/NTSB probes from the Jan child strike. London remains supervised testing only — a Sep commercial launch could not be confirmed as of Sep 8.

Trucking split into leaders and laggards. Aurora passed ~440,000 driverless miles with zero Aurora-attributed collisions, launched second-gen trucks, and reaffirmed 200+ driverless trucks by year-end with 2026 capacity fully contracted. Kodiak took a $100M PIPE at a steep discount (stock −37% on the day, May 7) and is still pre-long-haul-driverless. PlusAI pushed factory-built driverless to 2027. Gatik raised $200M for DC-to-store routes; Waabi demoed zero-retraining transfer onto a Volvo VNL. China went global and public: Momenta’s IPO, Baidu Apollo Go fully driverless in Dubai (23M+ cumulative rides across 28 cities, also testing in London with Uber and Lyft), WeRide at record margins in the Gulf, Pony.ai robotaxi revenue +691% YoY. Uber cemented itself as the aggregation layer — Dubai is the first city with multiple AV vendors on one platform. And Mobileye made the year’s most surprising strategy pivot: from supplier to vertically integrated robotaxi operator, own fleet launching 2027.

Defense & drones

New contracts and deployments expanded military use of autonomous aircraft, interceptor drones, and uncrewed boats. The examples below distinguish orders from deliveries and reported combat use.

Programs, procurement, and sources

CCA crossed from prototype to production. On Jun 17 the USAF awarded production contracts for both Increment 1 air vehicles — General Atomics’ FQ-42A and Anduril’s FQ-44A Fury — the first aircraft with an official “Fighter” designation and no cockpit, plus mission-autonomy contracts to six vendors (Anduril, GA, Lockheed, Northrop, RTX Collins, Shield AI). GA’s Apr prototype crash and six-week recovery became the showcase for fail-fast acquisition rather than a program-killer. Over 150 combat-capable CCAs are planned by decade’s end, ~1,000 eventually. Anduril separately closed $5B at $61B (May), unveiled its EagleEye soldier headset (Aug), and is reportedly shopping a new round at $100B — single-source, unconfirmed.

Replicator is dead; Drone Dominance is delivering. Replicator was formally dissolved in late 2025 (pre-window, missed by the May edition) into the Defense Autonomous Warfare Group with a ~$54B autonomy budget; CRS confirmed it delivered “hundreds, not thousands” of systems. The successor: a ~$1B two-year Drone Dominance program targeting 200,000+ one-way attack drones by 2027 — 25 Phase I vendors, 49 companies invited to Phase II at Camp Grayling, and >500,000 drones ordered through the new DoD marketplace with delivery times cut to 8 days. The Army wants lethal FPVs in every squad by Oct; the Marines held their first attack-drone competition at Quantico.

Ukraine kept rewriting the economics. Interceptor drones reached ~50% kill rates against Geran-2/Shahed-type drones, up from ~10% in winter — against a record ~8,150 Shahed launches in May (Jun: 5,749, 92% intercepted). Cheap interceptors are now the primary answer to mass attack, displacing missiles, feeding directly into US/European C-UAS procurement (AeroVironment’s $500M Army contract, Anduril’s Dutch win). Ukraine is also contracting for 25,000 UGVs in H1 and targeting 100% robotic frontline logistics.

Unmanned surface vessels had their combat debut. A Saronic Corsair recovered two downed US aviators off Oman (Jun 8) — the first publicly reported USV personnel recovery — and three Corsairs struck Iran’s Bandar Abbas naval base (Jul 12), the first US combat use of USVs. Saronic then picked Brownsville, TX for a planned $3B shipyard. European defense tech repriced violently: Helsing ($1.8B at ~$18B, delivering several hundred HX-2 strike drones per month to Ukraine), Stark (€500M at >€3.5B, Jun 23), Quantum Systems ($1.2B at $8B, Jul 2 — with a possible Quantum–Stark merger now openly discussed).

Drone delivery turned into a land-grab. In one 24-hour span (Jul 28–29): the FAA signed the nationwide Part 135 environmental FONSI (1,150 flights/day cap per hub), Wing/Walmart launched in Orlando, and DoorDash became the 8th US Part 135 drone air carrier with its own in-house program. Walmart passed 1 million cumulative drone deliveries (May 29); Zipline cited 2.5M+ commercial deliveries, 135M autonomous miles and 13× H1 growth in its marketplace businesses (Jul 13); Amazon announced a ~500-city Prime Air expansion (Aug 19); Uber and Zipline partnered with an aspirational 1M deliveries/day by 2029 (Aug 17). The catch: the Part 108 BVLOS final rule is still not final — in OIRA review since Jul 10, realistically Q4 2026–early 2027 — and the FCC is weighing retroactively revoking authorizations for existing DJI models (comments closed Sep 2).

The model layer

Robot AI is moving beyond tabletop tasks, and open-source evaluation tools are improving. Many new capability claims still come from the companies themselves.

Model and software developments

Whole-body crossed over. Google DeepMind’s Gemini Robotics 2 (Jul 30) does feet-to-fingertips humanoid control — demoed walking and manipulating on Apptronik’s Apollo 2 and tying knots on a 22-DoF SharpaWave hand — with an embodied-reasoning variant (ER 2), an on-device variant that adapts to new embodiments in hours, and a new ASIMOV-Agentic safety benchmark. Figure’s Helix S0 (Apr 29, pre-window edge) does sim-trained whole-body loco-manipulation with zero-shot stair traversal, and Helix-02 robots completed full 8-hour autonomous shifts (May; self-run, no third-party audit). In May, VLAs were tabletop; “whole-body” is now the frontier.

Adaptation cost is collapsing. Generalist AI’s GEN-1.5 (Aug 19) showed one-shot in-context physical learning — “physical prompting”: 59% average one-shot success across 10 tasks, 83% after 10 gradient steps on five minutes of data. Physical Intelligence’s π0.7 (Apr 16, pre-window edge) made generalist policies steerable via context conditioning. A week after GEN-1.5, Skild AI shipped S1 (Aug 25) with the same single-video in-context claim at longer horizon — one egocentric human video as the prompt, tasks up to ~10 minutes, a claimed 66% success on unseen tasks vs 9% for a language-prompted VLA — but with no paper, weights or API released; benchmarks are self-reported. Meanwhile world-action models took the benchmark crown: NVIDIA’s DreamZero → GR00T N2 (announced at GTC in Mar; GA promised end-2026, still pending) → Cosmos 3 (May 31, open weights). RoboArena’s #1 changed hands three times in four months — DreamZero (~1,750 Elo) → Cosmos3-Nano-Policy (1,881) → Spirit v1.6 (1,924, Jun 3) — a Hangzhou startup topping NVIDIA’s own benchmark cycle, the first Chinese model to lead it.

The open stack industrialized. Hugging Face shipped LeRobot v0.6 (Jul 7) — world-model policies, reward models, a 9-benchmark eval CLI — and patched CVE-2026-25874, a CVSS 9.8 unauthenticated RCE in its policy server (May 12): robot software now has a security track record. NVIDIA shipped Isaac Sim 6.0 GA (Jun 4) and Isaac Lab 3.0 beta, with the Linux Foundation Newton physics engine (NVIDIA + DeepMind + Disney) as a first-class backend — and announced the Isaac GR00T Reference Humanoid (Jun 1): an open reference hardware design on a Unitree H2 Plus chassis (~1.8 m, 31 DoF) with dual 22-DoF SharpaWave tactile hands and a Jetson AGX Thor T5000, expected late 2026 — the open stack now extends from weights to the chassis itself. ROS 2 got its new LTS, Lyrical Luth (May 22, supported to 2031). Chinese labs became the most prolific open-model publishers: Ant Group’s six-model LingBot stack (Jul), Unitree’s UnifoLM line, Xiaomi-Robotics-0. And then NVIDIA bought the platform itself: on Sep 3 it agreed to acquire Hugging Face for ~$13B, putting LeRobot, Reachy and the $399 Microduck (>$2.6M of orders in 24 hours) inside the incumbent. Data became a market in its own right: TRI spinout Walden Robotics emerged with a $300M seed (Jul 15), China poured >¥17B into 25 embodied-data startups in H1, and gig platforms (Instawork’s Instacore rigs, DoorDash Tasks) industrialized human-demonstration collection.

Geopolitics & supply chain

Trade rules and public policy now shape robotics markets. The US restricted some foreign-made networked robots, China introduced mandatory safety rules, the EU delayed parts of its AI Act timeline, and Korea and Japan announced national investment plans.

Policy and supply-chain details

The US drew a line. On Jul 28 the FCC added all foreign-produced “advanced robotic devices” — humanoids, quadrupeds, networked mobile ground robots over 4.4 lbs — to its Covered List (DA-26-786), blocking new models from FCC authorization and effectively barring them from US import/sale, with a Defense Department conditional-approval path and existing models grandfathered. The consumer fallout is real: nearly every robot vacuum, mower and pool robot is foreign-made, so Roborock/Ecovacs/Dreame’s US product cadence now routes through an FCC exemption process — and even US-assembled players (Matic) and Chinese-owned iRobot may be exposed. The House-passed FY2027 NDAA separately bars DoD from procuring adversary-linked humanoids. China threatened countermeasures.

China coupled deployment with binding rules. GB 44721—2026, the first mandatory national L3/L4 automated-driving safety standard, was approved Jul 30 (effective Jul 2027); a draft humanoid-robot standards system (Aug 25) targets ≥100 key standards by 2028 including ethics and social-impact assessment; MIIT/SASAC’s Real-Scene Training Special Action (Jun 9) ties 100+ deployment scenarios to SOEs. The state-procurement flywheel is now visible in order books — much of UBTech’s >¥1B in verifiable H1 orders is government/SOE tenders. Shipment data shows the lead: >97% of H1 2026 humanoid shipments were Chinese — but note the methodology conflict: SAG counts ~19,100 units globally under a strict definition, while a Chinese industry report claims >40,000 units (likely counting all embodied/wheeled robots). The two counts use different definitions and do not reconcile.

Europe softened its timeline. The Digital Omnibus (Reg. 2026/1744, in force Jul 27) postpones AI Act Annex III high-risk obligations from Aug 2026 to Dec 2027 and Annex I embedded-AI duties — the ones that capture machinery and robots — to Aug 2028, even as GPAI enforcement and Article 50 transparency went live Aug 2. Any May-era warning of an “Aug 2026 high-risk cliff” is now wrong. Capital, however, surged: NEURA’s up-to-$1.4B Series C and Humanoid Ltd’s $152M Series A (Jul 21, Europe’s first pure-play humanoid unicorn) closed the funding gap — with the caveat that NEURA still has zero verified named-customer deployments and the big Schaeffler deals (up to 2,000 Humanoid units; ≥1,000 Hexagon AEONs) only start installing in Dec.

Korea and Japan nationalized the race. Korea is now a three-way chaebol war: Hyundai took 100% of Boston Dynamics, Samsung was revealed building an in-house humanoid via a direct-to-CEO RX unit (Gauss model, Gumi “data factory”), and LG locked an NVIDIA GR00T biped for early 2027 — backed by ₩2.3 trillion of new government money through 2030. Japan published a METI AI Robotics Strategy (FY2026, ~¥90B/yr, 30% global-market target by 2040) and got its first explicit mass-production claim: Mitsubishi Motors × Highlanders (U-Tokyo spinout) converting a Kyoto engine line to build up to 1,000 humanoids/month from 2027.

The component layer became the bottleneck story. Inspire Robots shipped 10,000 dexterous hands in 2025; Chinese suppliers are pushing hand prices toward ~¥10,000 ($1,400); Harmonic Drive’s orders grew +56% YoY; Leaderdrive is racing reducer capacity from 50K to 100–120K units/month; Nabtesco is doubling RV output; and China is explicitly fusing its rare-earth magnet monopoly with humanoid manufacturing. That leverage now has a date attached: the Nov 2025 suspension of Beijing’s second-wave rare-earth export controls expires Nov 10, 2026, when the stricter traceability-and-licensing regime for heavy-rare-earth magnets (MOFCOM Notice 61’s 0.1% rule) snaps back — Western humanoid OEMs are qualifying non-Chinese magnet supply against that deadline. A May report asked “is there demand?” — the September question is who controls reducers, magnets and joint modules. The industrial cycle itself turned up: Japan’s robot orders set records for three straight quarters (JARA raised 2026 to a record ¥1.22T), FANUC posted record orders (+37%), North American H1 orders rose +2.0% in units/+6.6% in value (A3) with auto OEM orders down ~25% — growth is now non-automotive. Germany is the weak region (VDMA expects −5% in 2026).

Other sectors

Surgical, industrial, home, space, agricultural, and construction robots also advanced, though their deployment levels vary widely.

Updates by sector and sources

Surgical robots: another platform received US authorization. Johnson & Johnson’s Ottava received FDA de novo authorization on Jul 22. Medtronic’s Hugo is operating commercially in the US; CMR’s Versius Plus is beginning its US launch. Four soft-tissue surgical robot platforms now have US authorization, while Vicarious Surgical dissolved and Asensus retired its brand. Intuitive remains the market leader: it placed 468 da Vinci systems in Q2, including 246 da Vinci 5 systems, and reported an installed base of 11,710 da Vinci systems. On Sep 8 it announced CE-marked Force Feedback instruments and new digital features for da Vinci 5 in Europe.

Agriculture and construction: autonomous machines reached more customer sites. Bedrock Robotics said its excavators are operating without drivers at customer sites (Aug 17). Carbon Robotics reported more than $100M in revenue across about 20 countries and said it intends to pursue an IPO (Aug 31). Elbit acquired agricultural autonomy company Bluewhite (May 27). Komatsu commissioned its 1,000th autonomous mining truck in April. John Deere’s second-generation 9RX autonomy kit was still not available to order in early August.

Exoskeleton companies are consolidating. Wandercraft’s Eve received FDA clearance as a self-balancing personal exoskeleton (Aug 11); the company says its Calvin-40 humanoid has 12 industrial customers. Several Western exoskeleton makers have been acquired, entered insolvency, or shifted focus: Ekso is held for sale, German Bionic was acquired after insolvency, Lifeward raised convertible financing, and Sarcos moved toward defense AI. Chinese consumer exoskeleton maker Hypershell raised $50M (May 18) and launched a full-leg model at IFA.

Space robotics: lunar missions slipped, while in-orbit servicing advanced. NASA renamed and restructured its lunar robotics effort as “Moon Base” in March, with a plan for more than 20 robotic landings by 2029. Voyager acquired Astrobotic in July. Northrop’s Mission Robotic Vehicle launched in July and is operating in orbit. No lunar landings had been completed in 2026 as of September 8: Chang’e-7 was scrubbed on the pad Aug 23, while IM-3, Griffin-1, Blue Moon MK1, and Blue Ghost M2 were scheduled for late 2026 or 2027.

Home robots: hardware real, deliveries not. 1X has a factory and a sold-out first-year allocation but no verified NEO home deliveries; Sunday Robotics claims 99.1% zero-shot laundry folding across unseen homes (ACT-2, Jul 18) with a fall home pilot (participant counts unconfirmed); Weave’s Isaac units are actually folding laundry in California homes. iRobot’s comeback — two product waves at mid-market prices — is real but now runs as a Chinese-owned value brand under an FCC cloud.
